## 3 · Essay (Medium + Substack — same piece; set canonical to whichever you post first)

**Title:** Momentum, not inventory
**Subtitle:** Why "have everything" is the fastest way to kill a product in a crowded space.
**Tags:** AI, Startups, Product, Building in Public, MCP

---

There are around 10,000 MCP servers now. The official registry lists 9,652. On the skills side, the marketplaces hold over two million SKILL.md files.

I run a small product in this space. And a week ago, I set out to make sure it had all of them.

Then it hit me: that isn't the path to a great product. It's the path to a dead one.

**Completeness is a treadmill you lose slowly.**

Here's the uncomfortable math. If your pitch is "we have everything," you're in a permanent race against every other directory — including ones with more funding, more scrapers, and a full-time team whose only job is to be exhaustive. You will always be a little behind. And "a little behind, forever" is precisely what a slow death looks like. Nobody churns in a dramatic moment. They just quietly stop showing up, because the thing that was supposed to be complete… wasn't, quite.

Worse: completeness is a commodity. A list of 10,000 servers is worth exactly as much as the next list of 10,000 servers. There's no reason to pick yours. Aggregation, on its own, has no moat.

I actually already knew this. I'd even written a post titled "why AI tool directories are useless." And then I spent a week trying to build a better one. Founders are remarkably good at not taking their own advice.

**What a scraped list can't do.**

The thing that snapped me out of it was a simple question: what can a person with taste do that a scraper can't?

A scraper can tell you what exists. It cannot tell you what matters. It can't say "these three are worth your time, skip the other forty." It can't tell you what's *rising this week* — what just shipped, what practitioners are actually talking about, what's gaining momentum versus what's merely popular. It has no point of view, because it has no taste and no memory of the news.

That gap is the whole product.

**Momentum, not inventory.**

So I stopped chasing completeness, and made two decisions.

First, I automated "good enough." The directory now keeps itself reasonably current by pulling from the official registries — so I never manually chase another entry again. Baseline completeness became a boring background job, not a treadmill I run on.

Second, I put the real energy into the opposite of a directory: a weekly "what's actually moving" view. Not ten thousand things — the handful that are rising right now, tied to the news, refreshed daily. Momentum, not inventory.

The bet is simple. People don't come back to check a list that was complete yesterday and is complete today. They come back to find out what *changed*. Freshness is a reason to return. Completeness never was.

**This isn't really about MCP servers.**

The pattern generalizes to almost any product built on top of a commodity. When the raw material is abundant and everyone can get it, "we have the most of it" is the weakest position on the board. The moat moves up the stack — to curation, to taste, to freshness, to a point of view. To being the source people trust to tell them what deserves their attention, not the one with the longest list.

For a while I was afraid my product would fade, and I assumed the fix was to add more. The actual fix was to add judgment.

If you're building in a crowded space and you feel the pull to "have everything" — that pull is the trap. Be the one worth listening to, not the one with the most rows.

I put this into practice this week: a "This week in AI" view that shows what's gaining momentum instead of trying to be an encyclopedia. If you want to see it in the wild, it's on Kapyn — kapyn.app.

Momentum, not inventory. It makes for a better product — and, honestly, a much better way to spend the time you have to build.
